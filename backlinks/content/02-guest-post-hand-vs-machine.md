# Hand or Machine? How to Check Embroidery in 30 Seconds Before You Pay a Handmade Price

Search "hand embroidered t-shirt" on any big marketplace and you will find listings at Rs 499 sitting next to ones at Rs 2,499. Same flowers, same word "hand", five times the price. One of those sellers is either very generous or not telling you the whole story.

I work with a small team of women who embroider by hand in Bulandshahr, Uttar Pradesh. A simple name on a tee takes them a few hours. Our parakeet and floral tee takes 24 hours of stitching. A computerised embroidery machine sews a 5,000 stitch logo in about five minutes. Both are real embroidery and both have their place. The problem starts when one is sold as the other.

The good news is that you do not need training to tell them apart. You need 30 seconds and the inside of the garment.

## Step 1: Turn it inside out

This is the check that settles most cases. Machine embroidery is sewn with two threads: the coloured top thread you see, and a bobbin thread underneath that locks every stitch. On the back of a machine-stitched satin area, such as a letter or a leaf, you will usually see **a stripe of white or pale bobbin thread running down the middle**, with the coloured thread showing on both edges. It looks tidy, almost printed.

Hand embroidery uses one thread pulled through the cloth. On the back you see the same colours as the front, often in a looser pattern, with **small knots or short tails** wherever the stitcher started or finished a colour. You may also see long "travel" threads where the needle moved from one flower to the next without cutting the thread.

## Step 2: Feel for the backing

Machines need the fabric held perfectly still, so most machine embroidery has a **stabiliser** behind it: a piece of paper-like or mesh material, trimmed around the design. You can feel it as a stiff patch, and on a soft tee it can feel like a sticker sitting inside your shirt.

Hand embroiderers use stabiliser too, especially on stretchy jersey, but it is more often a soft, light layer, and some remove it after stitching. A stiff, square-cut patch with neat edges points strongly to a machine.

## Step 3: Look for twins

If a design repeats, say a row of hearts or two matching sleeves, compare them closely. A machine reproduces a digital file stitch for stitch, so the copies are identical: same number of stitches, same angles, same length. By hand, two hearts by the same person will be close but never identical. One petal sits a little wider. One leaf has a stitch that leans.

This is not a flaw. It is the signature of the method, and it is what people are paying for.

## Step 4: Read the stitch length

Run your eye along an outline. Machine stitches along a line are uniform, like a dotted line drawn with a ruler. Hand stitches vary slightly in length and angle, even in very neat work. Satin fills made by hand also tend to have a softer edge, because each stitch is placed by eye rather than by coordinates.

## Step 5: Ask the seller one question

"How many hours did this take?"

Makers know the answer, often to the half hour, because the time is the cost. A seller who answers "it depends" and then gives you a real range ("a single name is about three hours, a full floral front is closer to a day") is usually describing real handwork. A seller who cannot answer at all, or who says "two days" for a design that would take a week, is telling you something too.

## Watch the words in the listing

A few phrases are worth a second look:

- **"Embroidered"** on its own says nothing about how. Most embroidered clothing on the market is machine-made, and that is fine if the price reflects it.
- **"Hand-finished"** usually means a machine did the embroidery and a person trimmed threads or added a few details.
- **"Handcrafted look"** or **"artisan style"** describe a style, not a method.
- **"Hand embroidered"** with a very low price and same-day dispatch on a large stock is unlikely. Real handwork is usually made to order or in small batches. At our studio, custom pieces take 7 to 10 days.

## When machine embroidery is the right choice

None of this is an argument against machines. If you need 200 polo shirts with a company logo, machine embroidery is faster, cheaper and perfectly consistent, which is exactly what a uniform needs. Machine embroidery is also very durable when it is done well.

Hand embroidery makes sense when you want something a machine cannot give you: texture you can feel, variation that makes the piece one of a kind, and the story of the person who made it. It is a good fit for gifts, names, dates, small runs and anything you plan to keep for years.

The point is simply to know which one you are paying for. If you want a deeper comparison, including screen printing, our [hand vs machine embroidery guide](https://www.veethreads.com/blogs/news/hand-vs-machine-embroidery-vs-screen-print) breaks down cost, feel and how each one ages after a year of washing.

## A quick checklist to save

1. Inside out: knots and tails mean hand, a white bobbin stripe means machine.
2. Feel the back: a stiff, square-cut patch usually means machine.
3. Compare repeats: identical twins mean machine.
4. Check outlines: perfectly even stitches mean machine.
5. Ask for the hours. Makers know.

Use the checks together, not one alone. Some hand embroiderers finish the back beautifully, and some machine work uses a backing that washes away. Three or more signs pointing the same way are a reliable answer.
