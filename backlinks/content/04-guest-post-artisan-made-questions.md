# 64% of India's Artisans Are Women. Six Questions to Ask Before You Buy "Artisan Made"

"Handcrafted by women artisans" is on a lot of labels now. It is usually true. According to a Press Information Bureau fact sheet from December 2025, India has about 64.66 lakh handloom and handicraft artisans, and 64% of them are women. Among handloom weavers it is 71%.

So the phrase alone does not tell you much. Nearly any handmade brand in India can say it. What separates a brand that changes an artisan's life from one that just uses her photo is how the work is paid for, how regular it is, and whether her name and skill stay attached to it.

We run a hand embroidery studio in Bulandshahr, Uttar Pradesh, and we get asked versions of these questions by customers. Here are the six we think every buyer should ask, of us or of anyone else.

## 1. "Who made this, and can I see them?"

A brand that works with real artisans can usually name them, show their work and say how long they have been stitching. On our homepage you can meet Sunita dadi, who has done fine floral work for more than 25 years, and Rukhsana, who learned embroidery from her grandmother and now specialises in heritage motifs.

Anonymous "artisans" in stock photos are not proof of anything bad, but named people with specific skills are a good sign.

## 2. "Are they paid per piece, per day, or a salary?"

There is no single right answer. Per-piece pay can suit women who embroider at home around other work. Daily wages or salaries give more security. What matters is that the brand can tell you plainly.

For context, Uttar Pradesh's minimum wage for a skilled worker in districts outside the largest cities is Rs 15,224 a month from April 2026. That is a useful benchmark to compare against, even though home-based piece work is counted differently.

## 3. "How steady is the work?"

Irregular orders are one of the hardest parts of home-based craft work. A brand that sells all year, takes custom orders and plans collections in advance can offer steadier work than one that buys in bulk once a year.

## 4. "How long does one piece take?"

This tells you whether the price is honest. Hand embroidery is slow. One of our oversized tees, a parakeet and floral design, takes 24 hours of stitching. Makers on Reddit report 25 to 50 hours for a fully embroidered tee. A commercial embroidery machine sews a 5,000 stitch logo in about five minutes.

If a "hand embroidered" full-front tee costs less than a printed one, someone is either not being paid for the hours or the embroidery is not by hand. You can check the second one yourself by turning the garment inside out: hand embroidery shows knots and loose tails on the back, machine embroidery shows a neat stripe of white bobbin thread.

## 5. "Is it made to order?"

Real handwork is usually made in small batches or to order. Same-day dispatch on a large stock of "hand embroidered" pieces is possible for a big cooperative, but unlikely for a small brand. Our custom pieces take 7 to 10 days, and we say so on the product page.

Made to order also means less waste. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation estimates that one garbage truck of textiles is landfilled or burned every second, and that the average number of times a garment is worn fell 36% in fifteen years. Clothes that are made for a specific person, with their name or a date stitched in, are clothes people keep.

## 6. "Does the craft have a name and a home?"

India's embroidery traditions are specific. Lucknow chikankari, Kutch embroidery, Kasuti from Karnataka, Toda embroidery from the Nilgiris, Sujini from Bihar and Phulkari from Punjab are all registered Geographical Indications. A brand that knows which tradition its artisans come from, and says so, is usually closer to the craft than one that just says "Indian embroidery".

## Why this matters beyond one purchase

Embroidery is not a small, fading craft. EPCH figures show India exported about Rs 4,350 crore of embroidered and crocheted goods in 2024-25, up 9.12% on the year before, while handicraft exports as a whole were flat. Demand is growing. The question is whether the women doing the work share in that growth.
